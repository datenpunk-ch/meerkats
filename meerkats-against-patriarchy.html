Meerkats Against Patriarchy

A longform, figure-driven narrative: how cooperation, conflict, and maternal hormones complicate the myth that “power in nature” defaults to male rule.

Paper I — vocal ontogeny
01

The cry that shapes a life

In the southern Kalahari, a meerkat pup’s survival is measured in calls per second. From the moment it joins the foraging group at around 30 days old, it must compete — loudly, persistently, strategically — for food from adult helpers. Beg too softly and you go hungry. Beg just right and you gain the energy to grow, to learn, to survive.

But what if the intensity of that begging isn’t just a matter of hunger or personality — what if it’s already been written, weeks before birth, by your mother’s hormones?

“The pups weren’t just different animals. They were running different programs — and the code had been set in the womb.”

That’s what this three-paper research programme set out to test: whether maternal androgens — specifically testosterone — leave a lasting organisational imprint on how a meerkat pup develops its voice, its competitiveness, and its independence.

the experiment
Who the pups were

Three mothers, three fates

The study exploited a remarkable feature of meerkat biology: dominant females naturally have testosterone levels that exceed even the males in their group — especially during late pregnancy. Subordinate females have substantially lower levels. A third group was created by blocking androgen receptors in dominant females during the last three weeks of their 70-day pregnancy.

DC · dominant control

High-testosterone mothers

Natural dominant females. Their testosterone surpasses males during late pregnancy. Offspring exposed to the full androgen signal.

SC · subordinate control

Lower-testosterone mothers

Natural subordinate females. Still physiologically “masculinised” relative to most mammals, but significantly lower than dominants.

DT · dominant treated

Androgen-blocked mothers

Dominant females given the androgen-receptor blocker flutamide during late gestation. High rank, but offspring couldn’t receive the testosterone signal.

Methodology note

55 pups across 25 litters were tracked from emergence (≈30 days) to nutritional independence (≈120–130 days). 491 focal recordings were made using a directional microphone from less than one metre away. All statistical analyses used Bayesian multilevel models.

Paper I · finding
02

A robust trajectory vs a fragile one

Meerkat pups pass through three vocal phases as they grow: a period of full dependence (repeat calls, constant begging), a period of semi-dependence (digging calls, practising foraging while still soliciting food), and finally full independence (adult close calls, no more begging).

The question was: does maternal androgen status change how quickly pups move through these phases, and how sensitive they are to environmental conditions along the way?

≈30 days

All pups emerge and begin begging

DC pups — especially daughters — already show higher repeat call proportions. DT pups show the weakest early begging.

≈50–60 days

DT pups transition to digging calls — earliest, but not by choice

Their weak begging fails to secure adequate provisioning, pushing them toward foraging before they’re ready.

≈75 days

DC pups hit peak digging call proportions — and transition fastest

Having built up energy reserves from more effective early begging, DC pups move through semi-dependence efficiently.

≈88 days

DC offspring reach full independence

Significantly earlier than SC (≈110 days) or DT (≈125 days) offspring.

Crucially, DC offspring showed a canalised developmental trajectory — robust even under drought conditions or in larger, more competitive groups. SC and DT offspring showed greater plasticity: their vocal development was more sensitive to rainfall, group size, and competition.

Paper II — acoustic structure
03

How loud, how fast, how often

The second study went deeper: not just which call type a pup was using, but the fine-grained acoustic structure of each call. Duration, inter-call interval, and call rate — the rhythmic properties that determine how insistent, how urgent, how “expensive” a beg sounds to adult helpers.

The pattern was consistent. DC daughters produced the most intense begging: shortest calls, shortest gaps between calls, highest rates. DT daughters produced the least intense. And the effect was most pronounced in daughters — not sons.

The thesis

This isn’t just a story about meerkats. It’s a proof of concept: hormonal status can be a heritable social resource, passed from mother to daughter through biology, not behaviour.
