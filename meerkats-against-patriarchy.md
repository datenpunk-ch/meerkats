## Lede
Patriarchy is often presented as “natural”: a default social order that emerges wherever groups form, compete, and reproduce. But nature is not a single story. In the Kalahari, meerkats (*Suricata suricatta*) live in tightly coordinated groups whose daily survival depends on care work, negotiation, and constant attention to risk. Their social world is not a moral blueprint for humans—yet it is a powerful antidote to the idea that hierarchy must look one way.

This project uses a data-journalism lens to ask a simple question: **what happens to our assumptions about power when we look closely at a species whose social life is built on cooperation, distributed vigilance, and reproductive conflict that does not map neatly onto “male rule”?**

## What this story is (and isn’t)
This is not a “meerkats prove feminism” gimmick, and it’s not a nature documentary script. It’s an argument about **how we narrate social order**:

- We tend to treat “patriarchy” as a biological constant.
- We borrow metaphors from animals to justify human inequity.
- We ignore the fact that many animal societies organize power and care in ways that complicate the popular myth of the dominant male.

So the goal here is analytical: **to separate observation from ideology** and show—using figures, timelines, and a transparent methodology—where common claims about “natural hierarchy” collapse under scrutiny.

## Methods (transparent and readable)
This section should mirror your thesis methods, but written for a general audience.

### 1) From observation to events
We transform field notes into “events” (e.g., *sentinel bout starts*, *alarm call*, *pup feeding*, *aggressive displacement*), then aggregate them by day/week/season to compare patterns.

### 2) Measuring power without moralizing it
“Power” is operationalized as measurable control over:
- **access** (to food, grooming, proximity)
- **movement** (leading group travel or initiating departures)
- **reproduction** (who mates; who prevents mating)
- **sanctions** (who displaces; who is displaced)

### 3) Measuring care work as a system
Care work is operationalized as measurable investment in:
- **pup care** (babysitting duration, feeding events)
- **vigilance** (sentinel frequency and duration)
- **conflict dampening** (grooming / affiliative repair after aggression)

## Interpretation: what “against patriarchy” means here
The title is deliberately provocative. It doesn’t claim that meerkats “reject patriarchy” in a human political sense. It claims something narrower and more defensible:

- **“Patriarchy” is not a synonym for “biology.”**
- When people use animal stories to naturalize human domination, they cherry-pick.
- A close, data-grounded look at another social species reveals **multiple viable ways** to distribute risk, care, and control.
